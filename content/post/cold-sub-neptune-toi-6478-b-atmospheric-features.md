Well, here is a small but interesting piece of news. A new preprint, listed on [Astrobiology.com](https://astrobiology.com/2026/11/23/the-space-program-iv-atmospheric-features-on-the-cold-transiting-sub-neptune-toi-6478-b) as part of the SPACE Program series, reports atmospheric features on TOI-6478 b, a cold sub-Neptune. The paper is on arXiv as [2611.25230](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.25230), led by Anastasia Triantafillides, and has been submitted to the Astronomical Journal.

### Why this planet is special

Very few sub-Neptunes have a known atmospheric composition. The reason is that their atmospheres are often rich in heavy elements (high metallicity), or are covered by clouds and hazes. All of these flatten the spectrum and hide the molecules we are looking for.

TOI-6478 b is a cold case. Its equilibrium temperature is about 220 K (assuming no reflected light), it is roughly 4.5 times the radius of Earth, and its mass is below about 9.9 Earth masses, so it is quite fluffy. The [discovery paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.06848) found it orbiting a small, fully convective M dwarf every 34 days or so.

### What was detected

The team used the Hubble Space Telescope's WFC3 instrument to measure the transmission spectrum. During a transit, a little starlight filters through the planet's atmosphere, and molecules absorb it at specific wavelengths. The authors report an absorption feature near 1.4 microns, with a signal-to-noise ratio of about 6. They say this is the strongest spectral feature of any sub-Neptune seen with this Hubble instrument.

They also took care to rule out stellar activity as the cause, using a method that accounts for the transit light source effect (spots and bright regions on the star imitating a planetary signal).

Hubble's narrow wavelength window cannot tell whether the feature comes from water or methane. Self-consistent models favor methane, because at these temperatures water should condense and get trapped deeper in the atmosphere. Methane would fit nicely with the earlier prediction of strong methane features for this planet.

### My take

I would keep my excitement in check for now. This is a preprint, so it has not been peer reviewed yet, and I have only read the abstract-level details, so the significance of the detection still needs independent checking. Also, nothing here says anything directly about life. A 220 K planet with a thick hydrogen-rich envelope is not Earth-like.

Still, it matters. Temperate sub-Neptunes are the group where people ask whether some might be habitable, and we have almost no atmospheric data for the cold ones. The authors point to JWST, whose wider wavelength coverage should separate water from methane and measure the composition properly. That is what I will be watching for next.
