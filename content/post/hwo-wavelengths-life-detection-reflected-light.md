Well, here is a piece of mission planning that I find fascinating. A team led by Joshua Krissansen-Totton has asked what the Habitable Worlds Observatory (HWO) must be able to see for HWO life detection to be credible. Their paper, [Wavelength Requirements for Life Detection via Reflected Light Spectroscopy of Rocky Exoplanets](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14771), is on arXiv and has now been published in the journal Astrobiology, with a summary on [astrobiology.com](https://astrobiology.com/2026/11/25/wavelength-requirements-for-life-detection-via-reflected-light-spectroscopy-of-rocky-exoplanets-3). One caveat first: the results come from simulations, not from real observations of any planet.

## Why one gas is not enough

The headline idea is simple. Finding oxygen or methane in an exoplanet's atmosphere is not sufficient evidence for a biosphere. Both gases can, in some conditions, be produced without life, through photochemistry or geology. These are the false positives. There are also false negatives, where a living planet hides its signs.

So the telescope has to see more than the biosignature itself. It needs to learn whether the planet is habitable at all, and which other gases are present, so the context can rule out the non-living explanations.

## The wavelengths they recommend

For reflected light, meaning starlight bouncing off a planet, the authors recommend coverage from 0.26 micrometres (near-ultraviolet) to 1.7 micrometres (near-infrared).

- **The ultraviolet end, 0.26 micrometres.** This is needed to detect ozone, which can reveal a Proterozoic-like biosphere, an Earth of the past with much less oxygen than today.
- **The infrared end, 1.7 micrometres.** This lets astronomers place oxygen and methane in context by constraining carbon-bearing species in the atmosphere.

They also give rough targets for resolving power (about 7 in the UV, 140 in the visible and 70 in the near-infrared) and a signal-to-noise ratio of 20 to 40 across the whole range.

## My take

What I like here is the honesty. The authors say that not every Earth-like biosignature or false positive can be identified unambiguously, and that evaluating false positives might need different specifications from a plain biosignature survey. Still, they argue these minimal recommendations would allow a broad search for Earth-like life, assuming enough HWO targets can be observed this way.

My analysis: the chance of a clean "yes, this is life" from a single spectrum is small, but a planet with ozone, oxygen, methane and the right surrounding context would be a very strong lead. It is a preprint-turned-paper about requirements, not a discovery, so the thing to watch is whether these wavelength ranges survive into the final HWO design.
