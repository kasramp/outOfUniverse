## How LISA could find planets around white dwarf binaries

Most exoplanets we know were found with two methods: transits, where a planet dims its star, and radial velocity, where a planet makes its star wobble. Both need a lot of light from the star. Here is a third idea that I find fun, and it does not use light at all. According to [NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/lisa/gravitational-waves-can-reveal-new-worlds), two new studies led by NASA astrophysicists show in detail how the LISA gravitational-wave observatory could reveal a new population of exoplanets across the galaxy.

To be clear, this is a mission plan. No planet has been found this way, and [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/lisas-gravitational-wave-detections-could-reveal-exoplanets) points out that no planets are currently known around double white dwarfs at all.

## How the method works

LISA is an ESA-led mission with NASA contributions, planned for launch in the mid-2030s. It will use three spacecraft in a triangle with 2.5 million km sides to measure tiny ripples in space-time.

Its targets include double white dwarfs: two dead stars orbiting each other, some with periods under an hour. They emit gravitational waves at an almost constant frequency, like a steady musical note. Now add a giant planet orbiting the pair. The planet tugs the whole binary back and forth, and that motion adds a Doppler shift to the note. In the signal it shows up as faint sidebands at slightly higher and lower frequencies.

This is the same logic as radial velocity, only with gravitational waves instead of starlight. The idea goes back to Naoki Seto in 2008. The new work, published in The Astrophysical Journal, is a [paper by Casey McGrath](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/aea216) on which planet masses and orbital periods the method suits best, and a [second paper](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.3847/1538-4357/aea07d) on confirming such planets with the James Webb Space Telescope.

## The numbers

- The Milky Way probably holds about 10 million double white dwarfs with periods under an hour. LISA should resolve about 10,000 of them.
- Gravitational-wave signals fade with distance more gently than light does, so LISA could reach systems thousands of light years away.
- Under ideal conditions, LISA could detect planets down to about half a Jupiter mass.
- Webb could confirm a planet through its infrared glow out to at least 500 light years, but only for planets of roughly five Jupiter masses in the test case.

The test case was a known binary, ZTF J2243+5242.

## Limits

The authors call this an initial study. LISA's own motion adds a Doppler effect that has to be modeled carefully. A detection would only be a candidate until light-based observations, such as Webb or astrometry, confirm it. Planets that nobody knows about could also muddy the binary's signal.

## My take

These would be giant planets around stellar remnants, so they would not be habitable, and this is not about life. What I like is the reach. Transits and radial velocity struggle with such faint, distant systems, and this would sample a part of the galaxy we cannot study otherwise. It would also tell us whether planets survive, or form again, after their stars die. I will be watching for the first real LISA data, which is still about a decade away.
